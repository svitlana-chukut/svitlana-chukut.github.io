Organising the Delivery of Electronic Services in Ukraine During Wartime

Svitlana Chukut, Yevgeny Karpenko · 2023 · Public Administration and Regional Development, no. 20, pp. 589–613

Original Ukrainian article
Translation from Ukrainian under CC BY-SA 4.0.

Abstract

The article examines how national and local authorities in Ukraine organise electronic service delivery, with particular attention to the effects of war. It considers Ukrainian and international approaches to defining electronic services and analyses the United Nations e-government surveys, including the changes in the methodology of the 2022 Online Service Index. It identifies the index’s components, compares Ukraine’s Online Service Index and E-Government Development Index results for 2016–2022 and examines the main legislation governing electronic services. This includes laws on the protection of information in information and communication systems, electronic documents and document management, electronic trust services and the provision of public services, as well as the Cabinet resolution governing the national electronic-services portal and the Register of Administrative Services.

The study explores the benefits of electronic service delivery and the bodies responsible for it, including the Ministry of Digital Transformation, its Directorate for the Development of Electronic Services and the Intersectoral Council on Digital Development, Digital Transformation and Digitalisation. It considers services at three levels: the national Diia portal, mobile application and business platform, a sector-specific portal operated by the Pension Fund of Ukraine, and the Kyiv municipal service portal. The article examines how the war affected delivery and proposes directions for studying how to meet the needs of residents and businesses in wartime.

Keywords: e-governance, electronic services, Online Service Index, E-Government Development Index, digitalisation, ICT, Diia, Ministry of Digital Transformation of Ukraine, wartime services.

The problem in context

Using information and communication technologies in different areas of social life changes existing relationships and gives rise to new ones. Digital technologies are changing how individuals and businesses interact with national and local government. These changes reflect wider trends in public administration, including digital transformation, a greater emphasis on transparency and accountability, increased cooperation and partnership, recognition of diversity, and pursuit of the Sustainable Development Goals. Researchers and officials therefore focus on improving public electronic services through innovation and technology.

The Kyiv International Institute of Sociology found that 63% of adults in Ukraine, or roughly three in five respondents, used government electronic services during the previous year in 2022. Earlier studies found that 60% did so in 2021 and 53% in 2020 [1]. This shows a sustained increase in use, with a similar trend at the local level.

Given this increase, studying how services are organised is important to developing Ukraine’s electronic-service system. Research can build and organise knowledge, inform recommendations and help identify existing solutions to improve and new ones to introduce. It can also help prevent or reduce the effects of present and foreseeable risks.

Previous research and definitions

Electronic services are an interdisciplinary topic. Three common approaches are technological, service-oriented and user-centred.

The technological approach focuses on the infrastructure and tools used to provide electronic services, including the Internet, applications, mobile devices and communication networks. It views digital transformation as a way to make traditional services accessible, convenient and efficient through technology-mediated interaction. Brynjolfsson and McAfee [2] provide an example. Weill and Woerner [3] also examine the impact of digital ecosystems on services and the need for organisations to adapt their offerings.

The service-oriented approach emphasises the value proposition, customer satisfaction and mechanisms of delivery. It views electronic services as intangible offerings that provide utility and convenience. It draws on service marketing and prioritises user orientation, service quality and innovation. Vargo and Lusch [4] use service-dominant logic to explain why value is created jointly. Gummesson [5] likewise traces a shift from goods-dominant to service-dominant thinking and emphasises customers and the joint creation of value.

A user-centred approach starts with users’ needs, preferences, behaviour and experience. Services should be intuitive and easy to use, and they should meet users’ expectations and goals. User-centred design methods are used to ensure that services offer practical value and a meaningful experience. Norman [6] examines the principles of this approach and stresses usability and the user experience. Nielsen [7] sets out usability engineering principles and methods.

These approaches overlap. Researchers and practitioners often combine them to understand the significance and consequences of electronic services in light of the context, research aims and disciplinary perspective. Public-administration scholarship offers several further emphases.

  • The user-centred approach combines public administration, information systems and human–computer interaction. It examines how residents, civil servants and other stakeholders interact with electronic public services and what they need, prefer and experience. Examples include Dawes [8] and Mergel and Bretschneider [9].
  • The experience-oriented approach draws on user experience, interaction design and service design. It studies the emotional, cognitive and sensory aspects of citizens’ interactions with public digital services. Welch and Feeney [10] examine related dimensions of e-government.
  • The value co-creation approach brings together public administration, information systems and service science. Meijer and Bekkers [11] and Charalabidis, Koussouris and Askounis [12] consider how citizens and agencies create value together through the design, provision and use of services.
  • The ecosystem approach examines interactions, interdependence and relationships among agencies, citizens, businesses and civil society. The article discusses this perspective in connection with Lazer and colleagues [13].
  • The digital-transformation approach investigates how electronic services support changes in public administration. Norris and Moon [14] examine administrative efficiency, transparency and citizen empowerment, as well as strategic consequences of digital transformation.
  • An ethical and responsible approach examines privacy, data protection, transparency and accountability in e-government. Its representatives include Nissenbaum [15] and Zuiderwijk, Janssen and Dwivedi [16].

Studies also combine several approaches. The account of e-governance’s development in Ukraine by Chukut, Drahomyretska and Mykhailutsa [17] is one example. Other work examines service delivery at national and local levels and defines electronic services and their delivery arrangements. Ukrainian legislation and public-agency materials also offer definitions.

Electronic services have been defined as services provided using information and communication technologies and the Internet, including all public services delivered electronically through these technologies [18, pp. 12, 17]. Another definition emphasises interactions that are partly or fully mediated by IT [19, p. 429]. A shorter definition describes them as services provided electronically [20]. A public-sector definition describes an administrative or other public service delivered electronically to a person or legal entity, allowing them to obtain a licence, welfare assistance or identity document without visiting the responsible authority [21].

Ukraine’s law on public and electronic public services defines an electronic public service as one provided by national authorities, local authorities or organisations under their management. This includes administrative services delivered automatically or through information and telecommunications systems, with or without a request submitted electronically, including through the national portal [22]. Electronic delivery through modern information and telecommunications technologies is common to these definitions.

Four maturity stages are identified [20, p. 96].

  1. Publication. Government information is published on a website.
  2. Passive interaction. Citizens or businesses can initiate an electronic exchange but cannot complete it electronically.
  3. Active interaction. The citizen and government can complete the electronic exchange.
  4. Integrated e-government. Users can access complex, combined services.

The benefits include high-quality public services, greater trust in public authorities, lower administrative burdens, a more skilled and competitive workforce, better quality and accessibility for citizens and businesses, more efficient administration, encouragement of investment and innovation, and human-capital development [20, pp. 80–81]. Research on actual delivery arrangements complements work on these definitions, benefits and features.

Research aim

The article examines how Ukraine organises electronic services at national and local levels and how the war has affected that work.

Ukraine in the United Nations e-government surveys

The United Nations E-Government Survey measures the E-Government Development Index (EGDI) and the Online Service Index (OSI) [23]. The EGDI is a weighted average of normalised scores for online services, telecommunications infrastructure and human capital. The OSI quantifies online service provision across 193 UN member states. It standardises and normalises results by grouping questions into five areas: institutional framework, provision of services, provision of content, technology and electronic participation [23, p. 191].

Ukraine placed 46th out of 193 in the 2022 EGDI with a score of 0.8029, up from 69th and 0.7119 in 2020 [24]. Earlier results also indicate improvement. The original figures are reproduced here as data in a table.

Legal and institutional arrangements

A functioning electronic-service system requires a regulatory framework. Many laws and regulations address delivery directly or govern components of the system. Table 1 summarises the principal measures selected by the authors in chronological order.

Law on Electronic Documents and Electronic Document Management (2003)Establishes the organisational and legal basis for using and managing electronic documents and defines key concepts [26].

The Ministry of Digital Transformation is the central executive authority for e-governance and electronic services. The Cabinet directs and coordinates it under its resolution of 18 September 2019 [30]. The Ministry proposes directions for developing and reforming the delivery of electronic and administrative services; coordinates the work of authorities that set up administrative service centres; monitors service quality; and exercises statutory responsibilities for electronic identification and trust services.

Within the Ministry, the Directorate for the Development of Electronic Services develops policy papers and legislative proposals for the national portal and mobile application. It also participates in implementation, support and quality monitoring. It proposes policy options for identified problems, evaluates results, recommends implementation or adjustments, and assesses the resources required [31]. This directorate has direct responsibility for the service delivery system. Other units contribute within their own mandates.

The Intersectoral Council on Digital Development, Digital Transformation and Digitalisation is a temporary advisory body to the Cabinet. It examines implementation problems, assesses the state of digitalisation and e-governance, recommends policy and helps coordinate executive authorities [32].

Service delivery at three levels

Ukraine provides electronic services through a national portal, sector-specific government portals and local portals.

Diia and Diia.Business

The Ministry of Digital Transformation operates Diia, the unified national portal at diia.gov.ua [29]. National authorities, international organisations, private organisations and banks contributed to its development. Users can sign in through the National Bank’s BankID, the mobile application’s qualified Diia.Signature or a file-based or hardware key.

For individuals, the service catalogue covers eRobota, pensions and benefits, family matters, licences and permits, safety and public order, transport, land and property, certificates and extracts, the environment, health, identity and citizenship, and entrepreneurship. For businesses, it includes eRobota, transport, extracts and certificates, licences and permits, medicine and pharmaceuticals, land, construction and real estate, Diia.City and business formation [33]. The portal also publishes news, information on the portal and Ministry and frequently asked questions.

The Diia mobile application gives users access to digital documents. Examples include Ukrainian national identity cards, biometric passports for international travel, tax identification cards, driving licences and student cards. It also provides access to electronic services and can authenticate users on the portal.

The Diia.Business portal supplements Diia by providing business support and information for current and prospective entrepreneurs. Its tools include an entrepreneur’s guide, over 150 business ideas, legal templates for starting a business, Ukrainian case studies, news, free online and in-person consultations, the National Online School for Entrepreneurs, online exhibitions, a virtual business centre, export support, a financing marketplace, analyses of Ukrainian business and a platform to attract impact investment [34].

Pension Fund of Ukraine

The Pension Fund’s electronic-service portal at portal.pfu.gov.ua is an example of a sector-specific service. It lists five ways to authenticate: a qualified electronic signature, a secure signature token or Diia.Signature, ID.GOV.UA, an electronic pension certificate, and a username and password. The article notes the absence of a direct BankID option, despite wide use of banking services and the convenience and reliability of this method.

Separate sections serve pensioners, insured people, pension-insurance organisations, managers, associations, utility providers and social service centres. The portal publishes general information about the Fund, allows users to verify documents and offers a feedback form for rating in-person service from territorial branches.

City of Kyiv

The municipal portal at my.kyivcity.gov.ua offers a single entry point to information about Kyiv’s electronic services and uses a personal account to deliver services remotely [35]. Sign-in methods include mobile number and password, a Google account, Facebook or LinkedIn, a qualified electronic signature, BankID and Kyivstar’s MobileID. Some services require the higher assurance of a qualified electronic signature.

Services are organised under transport, family, registration, city council, social protection, architectural heritage, municipal payments, animal registration, electronic democracy, Ministry of Internal Affairs services, culture, sport and leisure. The portal publishes contacts, information and help materials.

Wartime effects on service delivery

The war tested the resilience of society and government, including electronic services. Its effects took two principal forms: restrictions on access to state registers and websites, and the creation of services in response to needs arising from Russia’s full-scale invasion.

Restrictions on registers and public websites

When the full-scale invasion began on 24 February 2022, Ukraine restricted access to many state registers to prevent theft or alteration of data during Russian attacks. Viktor Dubovyk, head of the Ministry of Justice’s office for countering raiding, reported attacks in the first hours on the State Register of Rights to Real Estate and the Unified State Register of Legal Entities, Individual Entrepreneurs and Public Formations [36]. Access was closed to protect the data. Other registers were also suspended, including the State Land Cadastre and the Unified State Register of Court Decisions. This affected public and business services that relied on those sources. As the situation stabilised and protections improved, registers reopened gradually. At the time of writing, most had fully or partly reopened and remained subject to suspension if threatened.

New services for households and businesses

Mass displacement from occupied areas and areas of active fighting made services for internally displaced people a priority. Diia could display an internally displaced person’s certificate, allow users to update their actual residential address or cancel their status, and accept applications for assistance and housing loans.

Military action destroyed or damaged many buildings, including homes. Owners could qualify for compensation to repair or replace housing. Documenting and reporting the damage was a prerequisite. The Damaged Property service digitised this process, allowing owners to record damage and submit an application.

Widespread job losses also prompted new electronic services. Applications opened for grants to start a business, establish an orchard or greenhouse, launch a processing enterprise, or support veterans and their families. These services were grouped under eRobota on the Diia portal.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study examined Ukraine’s organisation of electronic services at national and local levels, including legislation, public-administration bodies and practical tools for residents and businesses. Two forms of wartime response were identified. One restricted the operation of public registers and sites. The other created services for different user groups in response to new needs.

  1. Consolidate electronic services on Diia. Numerous portals make it harder for users to find and understand services. The authors recommend offering national and municipal services through Diia. An alternative is to move national services there and link to municipal portals.
  2. Standardise sign-in methods. Reducing the number of authentication methods and making them consistent across portals would help less experienced users and could avoid separate verification for sign-in methods with lower assurance, such as social media.
  3. Use emerging technology, including artificial intelligence. Chatbots could advise users on how to access services, answer questions and provide general information whenever needed. Cooperation with governments, civil-society organisations and businesses with relevant expertise is also a priority.
  4. Establish a secure environment for electronic public services that resists external interference and protects users’ personal data.

The analysis provides a basis for closer study of wartime effects on specific services and of how service delivery is organised. Its results can inform proposals for making electronic services more resilient in emergencies and for improving and expanding services in Ukraine more generally.
